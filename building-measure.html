    Using it

    1. Type an address and press Find. The map zooms to it and loads the building outline when one is on file.
    2. Check the outline against the photo. Drag a white corner to move it, click a dashed circle on a wall to add a corner, and right-click (or double-click) a corner to delete it.
    3. No outline, or the wrong building? Use Pick a building and click it, or Draw outline and click each corner (click the first corner or press Enter to finish).
    4. The front is set to the wall facing the address's street. Click any wall to change it. Width, depth and the side labels update to match.
    5. Use Measure distance for anything else, like setbacks or the distance to the street. Backspace removes the last point and Esc stops.
    6. Open the Compare tab to check the measurements against plans, and use Share to copy, download or print the results.

    Accuracy

    Measurements are calculated from map coordinates and are usually within about 1–2 ft for a carefully traced outline. Satellite photos show the roof, so roof overhangs (often 1–2 ft per side) make walls measure a little long, and tall buildings can look slightly shifted. Use these numbers for estimates and checks, not for legal surveys.

    Building outlines come from OpenStreetMap. Free satellite imagery is from Esri; Google imagery is used when a Google Maps key is added below.

    Shared development with Claude

    This tool was built with a shared workflow: a person sets the goal, Claude does most of the drafting and building, and people review and make the calls. The brief was a two-minute recorded call. Claude transcribed it, turned it into a list of requirements, built this page, and tested the address lookup and the measurement math before it went out for review.

    1. BriefGoal, examples, must-haves
    2. BuildClaude drafts a working version
    3. ReviewPeople test it and comment
    4. DeliverApproved version ships

    On a team, everyone does what they're best at. The project lead or client owns the goal and signs off on the result. Content contributors, such as writers, designers, estimators or surveyors, supply the copy, images, data and business rules through a shared folder or task tracker, without needing to code. The developer works with Claude in the code: Claude drafts features, writes tests, explains trade-offs and takes on the repetitive work, while the developer steers the direction, reviews every change and checks the result against the real need.

    The work runs in short loops. Each one ends with something people can actually use, a working page rather than a plan. Feedback comes back as comments or a quick call, Claude makes the changes, and version control (Git) keeps a history of every change so nothing is lost and anything can be rolled back. Because a working draft takes hours instead of weeks, the team can try a few options and keep the best one.

    People stay responsible for what matters: what gets built, whether the numbers are right, and what goes to the client. Claude doesn't publish, send or deploy on its own; a person reviews and approves every delivery. For this tool, a next loop could connect the company's Google Maps account, save projects so the team can share measurements, export a PDF for estimates, and check results against a few buildings with known dimensions to set an accuracy standard.
